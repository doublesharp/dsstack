# Write for the reader

Identify the reader, purpose, format, and voice from the request and surrounding text. Follow a supplied writing sample when available. For a first draft, use supported source material; for an edit, keep what already works.

- Lead with what the reader needs. Cut announced explanations, repeated conclusions, and empty praise. Keep caveats that change meaning, confidence, or the next action; remove boilerplate disclaimers, repeated qualifications, and unnecessary explanation.
- Name the action, behavior, or mechanism. Prefer familiar words and complete sentences. Remove filler without turning the text into clipped fragments or a slogan.
- Inspect paragraphs for repeated openings, forced groups, uniform sentence shapes, habitual em dashes, and stock "not just X, but Y" framing. State the point directly unless both sides of a contrast matter. Keep established terms and real three-item lists.
- Preserve facts, quantities, conditions, uncertainty, attribution, and the author's position. Do not invent examples presented as real, evidence, citations, personal experiences, opinions, or deliberate mistakes to add personality.
- Match the document's voice and purpose. Keep useful headings, tables, lists, humor, and punctuation. Follow the user's own style rules as given; treat generic AI-tell lists as prompts to inspect, not bans.
- Preserve code, commands, identifiers, paths, link targets, metadata, and exact quotations unless the task changes them.

Make one revision, then compare it with the source for lost meaning, unsupported additions, awkward phrasing, and a changed voice. Stop when the text serves its reader; do not chase detector scores or rewrite already-clear prose to meet a pattern count.

Return the finished text, or edit the requested file and summarize briefly. Give a critique or before/after analysis only when requested. An audit-only request ends with findings and leaves the text unchanged.
